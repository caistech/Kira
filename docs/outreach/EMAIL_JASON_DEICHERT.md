Jason,

That was the most useful feedback I've had from anyone in months, and you were right on every point.
So rather than tell you it resonated, here's what happened after I read it.

Kira has a domain: **https://kiraexec.com**. Favicon, proper metadata, the lot. It went live today.

That's the small one. The bigger one is your second and third points, which are really the same
point, and they're the ones that landed.

**On too many apps reading as unfocused** — you're right, and it isn't a portfolio strategy, it's
what happens when building is cheap and choosing is hard. So I've picked one. Kira is it. The others
either serve a paying client already (mmcbuild) or go quiet.

**On the marketing pages being clearly AI-written** — also right, and worse than you could see from
outside. My buyer is a business owner in his sixties, thirty or forty years in, thinking about
selling and usually having told nobody. That man has a very good ear for being sold to. Confident,
frictionless copy is exactly what makes him close the tab. I've since had the thing walked by
someone playing that persona and the findings were brutal in the right way — invented testimonials
that had to come out, a price that didn't say "+ GST", three pages that were blank for fifteen
seconds before anything appeared. All fixed, but none of it would have surfaced from the inside.

**On production being a different beast to a prototype** — yes. What has actually taken the time
isn't the features, it's the things that fail silently: the email that reports success and arrives
nowhere, the voice agent whose memory tools return nothing while every status code stays green, the
month of deploys that failed at install while production quietly served an old build. None of that
shows up in a demo.

**On selling an outcome rather than a tool** — this is where I'd genuinely like your view, because
I think I'm already closer to it than the pages suggest and haven't said it out loud. Kira isn't
sold as software. It's a project with an end: get what's in the owner's head onto paper so the
business can be sold or handed over for what it's actually worth. When that's done, she's largely
made herself redundant and the price drops to a third. The number it's priced against is the gap
between what the business is worth today and what it's worth documented — which for a plumbing
business we modelled came out at about $195k.

That's an outcome. I've been describing it as a tool. That's the gap, and it's the one you spotted
from the outside without knowing any of it.

**To your question** — no, I'm not selling modular units any more. I ran the manufacturing side in
Malaysia and Vietnam and the honest lesson was that the constraint was never production capacity,
it was approvals and finance on the other end. Which is roughly why I ended up building compliance
and feasibility software instead of factories. Happy to go through how that scaled properly if it's
useful to you — it's a longer conversation than an email.

On a deal: let me get one offer landing first. If Kira starts converting I'd rather come to you with
something proven and a real problem to solve than pay for calls into an offer I'm still sharpening.
But I'd like to keep talking either way — you told me things nobody else has, and you did it in your
own time.
