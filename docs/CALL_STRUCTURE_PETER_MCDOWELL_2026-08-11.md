## The problem you're actually solving

You've already offered Option 1 free: *"we take a look at your ops now and see where you and your
team are spending time."* That is an **audit** — it hands back a report, it sells a process, and the
owner has to bet on your judgement before you've proved anything. Peter said yes to it because he
likes you, not because the offer was good.

**Turn it into a diagnostic before Friday.** Same day of work, different thing entirely:

> An audit hands back *"here are some automation opportunities."*
> A diagnostic hands back **his number, from his systems, with a fix attached.**

The second one an owner grasps in a single read, and it sets the price of everything after it.

**He already uses Claude** — *"we use Claude to do a similar thing, but happy to compare."* So the
differentiator is not that AI can help. He knows. It's that you find money his own stack is hiding
from him, and most of those findings have no AI in them at all. Say that out loud; to a man already
using Claude it is far more persuasive than a capability list.

**Do not pitch Kira.** He chose Option 1, and pitching Kira into an Option-1 conversation is exactly
what produced *"I am not even sure what the product is."* One sentence if he asks.

**Open with this, because it's true and checkable:** his 5 August message is quoted verbatim in the
codebase (`scripts/send-owner-invite.mjs:52`) as the reason the new-owner email was rewritten — and
that rewritten email went to three real business people today. He gave feedback and it changed the

## Tuesday's only job

**Decide which two or three numbers you go and check on Friday.** Nothing is solved on this call. If
you leave with a target and agreed access, it worked.

### 1. One job, end to end (10 min)
Not "tell me about the business" — one real job.
- *"Take the last job that finished. First contact to the money in the account — who touched it, and
  where did it sit waiting?"*
- Listen for **the queue**, the step where things rest. The leak lives there, not where the effort is.
- *"How many a week, and who else can do the whole thing without you?"*

### 2. Which system holds the truth (10 min)
You're hunting for two versions of one number.
- *"Which report do you look at most?"* → **then the money question** → *"and which number on it do
  you not quite believe?"*
- Accounting, quoting/CRM, job tracking, where email lives, what's still spreadsheets or in a head.
- *"When you want to know what you actually made last month, where do you go — and how long before
  you trust the answer?"*

### 3. His theory (5 min)
Ask outright, write it verbatim. It's usually the loud thing and usually wrong — and either way it's
useful: right means you confirm fast, wrong *is* Friday's finding.
- *"If I told you that you were losing money somewhere, where would you guess?"*

### 4. Claude, straight on (5 min)
He raised it; dodging reads as defensiveness.
- *"What are you using it for, and what hasn't it touched?"*
- *"Who's driving that internally?"* — *"how our 'Claude' is going to feel"* implies somebody owns it.
  Champion or rival, you want them in the room Friday.

### 5. If he took four weeks off (5 min)
The key-person question, without naming the product.
- *"If you were out for a month, what breaks first — and who rings you anyway?"*
- This is the Kira wedge. Let him say it. Don't close on it.

### 6. Scope Friday and get the access (10 min)
**The call fails if it ends without this.**
- Name the two or three numbers, and which system each comes from.
- **Read-only access, or him at the keyboard for an hour.** Decide now, not on the day.
- Who else is there.
- What he gets: one page — the number, where it came from, the fix.

## Friday: the three moves that produce the number

1. **Check every reported number against the system that holds the truth.** Dashboards report on
   themselves. Pull the same metric from the source of record and compare. A gap is the fastest
   credibility available — you've just told an owner something his own stack was hiding.
2. **Find where people actually drop, not where he thinks they drop.**
3. **Name the fix even when it isn't yours to sell.** Handing over a free fix is not lost revenue; it
   is what makes everything you propose afterwards credible. It proves you're aimed at his P&L rather
   than your invoice.

## What you offer at the END of Friday — the bit the audit version never gets to

The finding sets the price. You don't price the build, you price the leak, and he does the arithmetic
himself before you open your mouth. Three shapes, decided by what you find:

- **Nothing material.** Say so plainly and stop. That single act of not-selling is worth more to your
  name in his network than a small engagement, and he will repeat it.
- **A fix he can do himself.** Hand it over free. Then: *"the same look across the other three parts
  of the business is a week's work"* — priced, scoped, one page.
- **A fix that needs building.** Price against the leak, not the hours. *"This is costing you $X a
  month; the build is $Y once."*

**What you're really collecting is a case study.** The register says nothing is validated by a
customer, and Neil closed his call asking for references and heard "still building" three times.
Peter is an owner-operator in the ICP, he trusts you, and the look is already free. Ask for
permission — even anonymised — to say *"a Perth business we looked at."* That sentence is the thing
you didn't have yesterday.
